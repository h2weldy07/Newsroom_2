Humans vs. Robots in the Battle for the Future of Music

AI has become essential to a lot of people in their daily lives. Whether you are asking a chatbot to write you an essay that you’ve procrastinated for your English class, solve a complicated math problem, or act as a free personal therapist to give advice on your messy love life. AI can make our lives easier by simplifying tasks that otherwise would have taken up an unnecessary amount of time to complete. But where do we cross the line from taking simple shortcuts into starving our own creativity? The use of artificial intelligence in the music industry has become a point of major controversy in the past few years. According to findings by Gabrielle Chou and Nicolas Lang in the global blog ARTEFACT, platforms like Suno AI, an artificial intelligence music generator with lyrics, allows people to make entirely new songs with a simple prompt. The app allows you to make music and curate a specific mood in whatever genre you want in under a minute. Various other websites like AIVA, Endel, Boomy AI, and MuseNet have encouraged this idea as well. But this rise in artificial music creation is encouraging less creativity and human connection, something that music has always been centered around.

Meaning is brought to music through the unique experience of the people writing and singing the songs. Oversaturating the industry with AI generated music can come across as an insult to those who have made it their entire career. Yes, anyone can create music, but many people are arguing that the value in authenticity is lost when artificial intelligence can generate an entire song from a few words typed into a computer. If you’ve ever had a burst of inspiration that led you to try to write a song with a friend at a sleepover or in your room after a rough day, then you know that it actually takes a lot of effort to make good music. Writing the lyrics, recording the instruments and vocals, and producing the tracks all require specific skills that people develop over time spent working diligently on their craft. Artificial intelligence tries to capture this same effect without any of the human process behind it. So naturally, it can easily fall short.


AI has made an already overcrowded industry even more difficult to compete in. Not only do music artists have to contend with other talented musicians trying to break into the industry, but they now have to deal with artificially generated competition as well–as if the chances of being the next Justin Bieber that gets discovered on Youtube weren’t already bad enough. Imagine watching as a song produced entirely by a robot gains more popularity than one that you spent months crafting lyrics and chord progressions for in your notebook–not an ideal situation for artists trying to get their name out there.


Aside from getting recognition, artists have growing concerns over what this means for how much they are getting paid. “Who owns what?” has become a difficult question to determine. For the most part, AI can only reuse information that has already been previously put out into the world, it lacks the lived experience to produce completely original works. But if artificial intelligence uses past data to train its models, how did it get its music abilities?


This is the problem being faced by artists today. When a song is created using AI, the ownership cannot be credited to one specific artist because the model was trained across millions of songs. AI music apps are essentially being trained on unlicensed datasets, so it is no longer as easy as identifying who wrote the song and paying royalties accordingly. Artificial intelligence understands and generates music first through a process called tokenization. This is where music is broken down into more manageable chunks. These apps like Suno AI and MusicLM are then able to analyze musical structures in order to create new complete compositions much faster than a human ever could. Because these models are learning how to make music by analyzing real artists' voices, they can’t really claim them as their own. According to Forbes, since this is a relatively new development, copyright laws do not exactly cover this type of issue when it comes to originality. So royalty streams for human artists are decreasing and streaming apps are saying AI music is “good enough” if it can save them money in the long run. This puts artists in a tough spot.


According to billboard.com, in September of 2026, both Sony Music Entertainment and Universal Music Group filed a lawsuit against Suno AI for using their copyrighted music to train AI models. This came after an initial lawsuit filed in June of 2024 detailing 560 copyrighted sound recordings that Suno AI used without permission from either company. Both companies argued that Suno is “saturating the market” with arguably subpar music, taking away from the talent of real people. At its core, artificial intelligence doesn’t make actual “good” music. But if bad music is inevitably going to be put online, it should at least come from a real human and their own original ideas.


This doesn’t mean that artificial intelligence hasn’t contributed any positives to the music industry. Spotify uses algorithms to analyze your listening habits and suggest tracks. They offer daily mixes and customized playlists based on your previous listening history. In this way, people are exposed to a broader range of music that they might not have discovered otherwise and can have a more personalized listening experience on the app. Artificial intelligence in this way has actually improved the quality of user experience for Spotify and other music apps.


Ultimately, steps need to be taken in order to prevent the influence of artificial intelligence from taking over the music industry. This ongoing struggle has certain big name celebrities in the business taking a stand. According to The Guardian, in 2024, over 200 music artists signed a letter put out by the Artists Rights Alliance against the development of AI tools that would replace human songwriters. Several big names in the industry signed the letter including Billie Eilish, Nicki Minaj, Stevie Wonder, and even the estates of massively influential artists Frank Sinatra and Bob Marley. The letter stated that AI should not be using the work of human artists in order to end up replacing human works. The call for regulations in the industry has largely gone unnoticed in the past two years since the letter was made, but platforms like Spotify have taken steps to prevent AI from going further. The app removed over 50 million AI generated music tracks from its recommendations section per complaints by users, but kept them as reachable through search on the platform. Spotify also released an AI badge that would be placed on any music that was flagged as AI through their review process so that listeners are aware of what they are streaming.


Technology has continued to change the way that we create music, and has made huge strides in improving the way we record and produce songs. But finding a way to make music without musicians should never be the goal of artificial intelligence. Chatbots are not artists, as many prominent celebrities and passionate musicians have argued in the past few years. More rules and regulations need to be placed on AI to keep real voices at the forefront of the music industry because, let’s face it, humans just know how to make a better song.
